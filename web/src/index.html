Could autonomous shuttles carry a whole town's bus rides?

Singapore is starting driverless shuttles inside new towns. This study asks what it would take in a mature one. It replays every bus ride Yishun residents tapped in and out of inside their town, through a simulated fleet, on a road network calibrated to live bus GPS.

Why this question

Singapore's driverless plans are shuttles inside towns, not private cars

Car ownership in Singapore is capped by the vehicle quota, so the autonomous future being tested here is fleets serving towns. Punggol's autonomous shuttles opened to the public on 1 April 2026 to "enhance connectivity to key amenities and transport nodes" (LTA, 4 March 2026). By July more than 11,500 people had ridden, and Grab began piloting on-demand, direct-routing rides between pick-up points (LTA, 29 July 2026). In October, LTA paused its autonomous public-bus pilot to explore narrower uses first (Mothership, 13 October 2026).

Punggol is a new town with a small pilot fleet. Yishun was built mostly in the 1980s and has about 221,600 residents (Census 2020). Its residents make about 158,000 bus rides a day without leaving it. This study takes the on-demand model being piloted in Punggol and asks what it would take at that scale.

What this study is not

It does not forecast AV adoption, fares, costs or safety, and it is not an LTA plan. Demand is held at today's observed bus rides. It compares two ways of serving the same rides on the same roads, and measures what the comparison would cost in vehicles, kilometres and kerb space.

Where the brief went instead

The starting brief proposed simulating 20–80 % private autonomous cars in traffic, with roadside V2X units and new signal strategies. That frame does not fit Singapore. Car numbers are capped, signals already run on LTA's adaptive GLIDE system, and car-following models would mostly echo their assumed headways. Junction counts suitable for calibration are sparse one-day surveys. Fare-card data, by contrast, gives every observed bus ride inside the town.

The trips

Yishun's internal bus rides are short and point at the MRT

LTA publishes, for each month, how many fare-card rides went from every bus stop to every other, hour by hour. Keeping only rides that start and end inside the Yishun planning area leaves — rides on an average weekday of September 2026, across — stop pairs.

Half of them cover less than — as the crow flies, and — start or end at Yishun or Khatib MRT or the bus interchange. These are feeder trips: a few stops between an HDB block and the train. For trips this short, waiting is a large share of the journey. That is where an on-demand vehicle could help, and also where it has the least room for error.

Finding 1 · the fleet

Shuttles beat the bus easily, until the fleet is a few dozen vehicles short

Each simulated day sends every observed ride through a fleet of pooled, on-demand shuttles that stop only where riders board or alight. Today's buses are modelled from LTA's routes and published headways, with running times, signal delay and dwell calibrated on — live bus observations, and waiting times from measured headway regularity.

Above a threshold the shuttles roughly halve the bus's stop-to-stop time, because riders wait less and ride direct. Below it, the morning peak builds a queue that the fleet cannot clear for hours. The edge is sharp: a fleet — vehicles smaller than the one that copes can lose the whole morning.

Each point is the mean of three demand replicates; the band spans them. Vehicle size matters less than vehicle count: occupancy at the threshold is about two riders.

Finding 3 · the kerb

The MRT kerb becomes the scarce infrastructure

Two-thirds of rides touch a station, so a pooled fleet converges on a few hundred metres of kerb. With the fleet that matches the bus all day, up to — shuttles are stopped at once at the Yishun MRT and interchange stops, and — at Khatib, before counting idle shuttles waiting for work.

At about 10 m of kerb each, that is a long platform at the busiest place in town. The design question moves from "how many vehicles" to where they stop, and how riders walk to them.

Method

What is observed, what is modelled, what is assumed

observed Data

  • Rides between every pair of stops by hour: LTA DataMall fare-card OD, Sep 2026.
  • Bus routes, stop sequences, first and last buses, headway ranges: LTA DataMall.
  • Bus running times and headway regularity: — matched observations from LTA's live arrival feed, 15 Oct 2026, —.
  • Roads: OpenStreetMap. Land use and boundaries: URA Master Plan 2019. Block storeys: HDB.

modelled Model

  • Road travel time = calibrated cruise time + — s per signal-controlled location; bus dwell — s per stop (+— s at stations). Same roads and speeds for both modes.
  • Bus wait = — × combined headway of the lines serving a stop pair (common-lines model).
  • Shuttles: event-driven simulation, immediate insertion dispatch, rides within 1.6× direct + 4 min, promises slip at most 5 min, idle vehicles reposition towards expected demand. Riders who would wait over 45 min are counted as refused.

assumed Not modelled

  • No change in demand: riders, times and stops are today's. No fares, no mode shift, no induced trips.
  • No traffic interaction: hundreds of shuttles do not slow each other or the buses. Speeds are calibrated on late-morning data; peak congestion is not.
  • No kerb capacity limit, no charging, no breakdowns, no safety operators. Bus crowding and denied boarding are not modelled.

make experiments   # fleet x capacity x demand replicates
make web           # export data for this page
