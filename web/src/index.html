About this study and what it assumes

Singapore is starting driverless shuttles inside new towns. This study asks what it would take in a mature one. It sends a simulated weekday of Yishun's internal bus rides, drawn from LTA fare-card counts between every pair of stops, through an on-demand shuttle fleet and compares every ride with today's buses. Road times are calibrated to LTA's live bus data and checked against bus GPS movement.

Four-seat vehicles would need —, twenty-seat ones —. The dispatcher matters as much as the vehicles: one that considers four times as many shuttles per request needs only —. Across — alternative assumptions the ten-seat fleet ranges —–—. Several assumptions favour the shuttles: unlimited kerb space, no traffic added by the fleet itself, no charging, travel times known exactly, and road speeds measured outside the morning rush. Two favour the bus: crowding is ignored, and the dispatcher is a simple one.

Why this question

Singapore is testing driverless fleets inside towns, not private cars

The background, and what this study is not

Car ownership in Singapore is capped by the vehicle quota, so the autonomous future being tested here is fleets serving towns. Punggol's autonomous shuttles opened to the public on 1 April 2026 to "enhance connectivity to key amenities and transport nodes for residents within Punggol" (LTA, 4 March 2026). In September the Transport Minister said Singapore had "only 16 passenger vehicles, all in Punggol" and about 20,000 riders so far. Next comes a trial of point-to-point service "within the whole estate, without the need for fixed routes", then one to two more HDB towns, Tampines North and Tengah (MOT, 18 September 2026). In October LTA stopped a planned pilot of autonomous buses on two public bus services, to look at narrower uses first (Mothership, 13 October 2026, confirmed by LTA).

Yishun was not among the towns named. Three in five of its HDB blocks were completed in the 1980s (HDB property data), it has about 221,600 residents (Census 2020), and about — bus rides a day start and end inside the town. In Punggol, Grab is piloting on-demand rides "between existing pick-up and drop-off points", taking the most direct path (LTA, 29 July 2026). This study asks what that model would take at Yishun's scale. Pickups and drop-offs stay at today's bus stops, so walking is the same in both modes.

What this study is not

It does not forecast AV adoption, fares, costs or safety, and it is not an LTA plan. Demand is held at today's observed bus rides. It compares two ways of serving the same rides on the same roads, and measures what the comparison would cost in vehicles, kilometres and kerb space. The closest Singapore study, Mo et al. (2021), simulated shared autonomous vehicles competing with buses for first-mile trips in Tampines, with operators choosing fleets and fares. This one holds demand fixed and asks what replacing every internal bus ride would take.

Why not simulate self-driving private cars?

An earlier plan for this project was to simulate 20–80% self-driving private cars in Yishun's traffic, with roadside radio units and new traffic-signal plans. That does not fit Singapore. Car numbers are capped, traffic signals already adapt to traffic, and simulated self-driving cars mostly reproduce the following distances they are given. Open junction counts are sparse one-day surveys, while fare-card data counts the bus rides inside the town.

How the rides are counted

LTA publishes, for each month, how many fare-card rides went from every bus stop to every other, hour by hour. Keeping only rides that start and end inside the Yishun planning area leaves — rides on an average school-term weekday (July and August 2026), across — stop pairs. Half of them cover less than — as the crow flies, and — start or end at an MRT station (Yishun, Khatib or Springleaf) or the bus interchange. These are feeder trips: a few stops between an HDB block and the train. For trips this short, waiting is a large share of the journey. That is where an on-demand vehicle could help, and also where it has the least room for error.

LTA counts fare-card rides by boarding and alighting stop. Riders tap in and out, and LTA does not document how the few rides without a tap-out are handled. A trip that changes buses inside Yishun counts as two rides. Riders may live anywhere; what is counted is where they board and alight.

How the kerb is tested

Two-thirds of rides touch a station, so a pooled fleet converges on the stops around each MRT station. In the base case kerb space is unlimited, and the model counts what the fleet uses. With the fleet that matches the bus all day, up to — shuttles board or alight at once at the Yishun MRT and interchange stops, and — at Khatib, before counting idle shuttles waiting for work.

The model does not know how many vehicles each stop can hold. The kerb test asks how much it would need. The design question moves from "how many vehicles" to where they stop, and how riders walk to them.

In the kerb test, a shuttle that finds every berth at its station taken queues until one frees. The dispatcher does not anticipate these queues, so pickups there can slip beyond the usual five-minute limit.

Method

What is observed, what is modelled, what is assumed

observed Data

  • Rides between every pair of stops by hour: LTA DataMall fare-card OD, July and August 2026 (September as a check).
  • Bus routes, stop sequences, first and last buses, headway ranges: LTA DataMall.
  • Bus running times and headway regularity: — matched pairs of LTA's live, GPS-based arrival estimates, 15 Oct 2026, —.
  • Bus GPS movement: — pairs of reported positions of the same bus a few minutes apart. They come from the same feed and day, but are observed positions rather than LTA's estimates.
  • Roads: OpenStreetMap. Land use and boundaries: URA Master Plan 2019. Block storeys: HDB.

modelled Model

  • Road travel time = calibrated cruise time + — s per signal-controlled location; bus dwell — s per stop (+— s at stations). Same roads and speeds for both modes.
  • Bus wait = — × combined headway of the lines serving a stop pair (common-lines model).
  • Shuttles: event-driven simulation, immediate insertion dispatch, rides within 1.6× direct + 4 min, promises slip at most 5 min, idle vehicles reposition towards expected demand. A stop takes — s plus 4 s per rider (assumed). Riders who would wait over 45 min are counted as refused.

assumed Not modelled

  • No change in demand: riders, times and stops are today's. No fares, no mode shift, no induced trips, no merging of two-bus trips into one.
  • No traffic interaction: hundreds of shuttles do not slow each other or the buses. Speeds are calibrated on 10:00–13:00; the morning rush was not observed. In the evening peak, LTA's estimates imply buses —× slower than the midday model, while bus GPS positions show —× (95% interval —). One case in the sensitivity table applies the evening-peak calibration all day.
  • The dispatcher knows travel times exactly. No kerb capacity limit in the base case (a limit is tested separately), no charging, no breakdowns, no safety operators. Bus crowding and denied boarding are not modelled.
